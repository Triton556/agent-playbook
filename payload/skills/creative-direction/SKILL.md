# Creative direction

Develop a creative direction around the experience the user wants. Alternatives, references and sketches are available ways to clarify that direction, not required stages or output quotas. An already clear idea can proceed directly to execution.

The user may bring a direction from Claude or from their own work. If a creative brief or an accepted direction already exists, it is the basis: execute and refine it, do not regenerate alternatives unless asked.

Separate two kinds of input. **Things the user likes from others** (a game, a film, a product) are references: extract the principle and do not copy their surface. User material: see [task brief and acceptance, section 2](../dev-flow/references/task-brief-and-acceptance.md#2-understand-the-intent). In addition to that preservation rule, raise a creative risk once with a solution inside the user’s idea. Domain guidance: [UI/UX](references/ui-ux.md), [content](references/content.md), [game design](references/gamedev.md), [visual, motion and 3D](references/visual.md). Open the one that matches the deliverable; a mixed task opens each.

## 1. Find the principle, not the surface

When the user names things they like (a game, a film, a product, a past result), use what they have said to identify the experience or quality they value: what a person feels, does or understands. A question about a specific example can help if the intent is unclear. Distinguish that principle from the reference's surface: its setting, signature mechanic, layout or style.

Example: "I loved the map in a metroidvania" can mean "earning orientation in a hostile place" or "the pleasure of seeing a blank area fill in". Neither requires a map screen. A direction that rebuilds the same map system is a reskin, not an idea.

A concrete memory ("which moment made you want to continue?") can be more useful than an abstract either/or.

## 2. Explore alternatives when useful

If alternatives would help resolve an open choice, use the domain axes to explore meaningfully different ways to deliver the experience. A straightforward idea is valid when it fits the goal; novelty is not an acceptance criterion by itself.

## 3. Explain a proposed direction

Describe a direction concretely: a moment from the user's, player's or viewer's side, why it fits, and any material cost or risk. When comparing alternatives, explain the meaningful tradeoff rather than changing only theme or decoration. The number and presentation follow the actual question, not a prescribed format.

## 4. Use references as an optional aid

An example or a reference from the user may help clarify an uncertain intention or taste. You can offer a relevant example or ask for one when that would help; an existing brief or the user's explanation may already be sufficient. If the user reacts to examples, retain the concrete qualities they mention rather than substituting generic adjectives. References are not a prerequisite for continuing.

## 5. Sketch when it helps a decision

A wireframe, style frame, short script, paragraph of play or rough blockout can make an unresolved visual or experiential choice easier to assess. Use a sketch when it provides useful evidence for that choice, rather than producing one for every direction by default. Label exploratory work as such.

## 6. Hand over a creative brief

Record a settled direction in the existing plan and continue with the execution skill for the domain. Reuse an existing brief; include the details that matter to this task:

- Goal and audience, in the user's words.
- Principle and the surface not to copy.
- Chosen direction as its moment; what must survive into the final result.
- Relevant references and reactions, if any; constraints (design system, brand, platform, budget, scope).
- What not to do; open questions.

The brief is the same whether Codex, Claude or the user wrote it. Later stages check the result against it.

## Check the proposed direction

- Is any direction a reskin of a named reference?
- If alternatives are offered, do they differ meaningfully rather than only in theme, color or wording?
- Is each one described as a concrete moment someone experiences?
- Is the direction grounded in the user's expressed intent rather than an assumed taste?
- Preservation: see task brief and acceptance, section 2.
